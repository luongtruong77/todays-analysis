Seattle Seahawks win by 10+

If the Seahawks dominate

SEA 33–15 CHI 32% chance · avg margin 18.3

Mike Macdonald's defense, the best in the league by EPA allowed per dropback (−0.137), does what it does to most offenses. It disguises pressure, Caleb Williams (#18) holds the ball, and the Bears go three-and-out or turn it over. Seattle's offense has been mediocre (−0.04 EPA/play), so the blowout path runs through Jaxon Smith-Njigba (#11) winning early against an average Chicago secondary, short fields from takeaways, and then a fresh three-back rotation grinding the clock in the second half. Chicago spends the fourth quarter throwing into soft coverage.

What has to happen

  • Josh Jones (#74) holds up at left tackle so Sam Darnold (#14) stays clean. Pressure is what turns Darnold's game into turnovers.
  • SEA wins the turnover battle: Chicago's defense is opportunistic, but Caleb against a disguised rush is the likelier source.
  • Smith-Njigba gets his usual 11+ targets with Shaheed out, and the red-zone offense (54.5% TDs) finishes drives.
  • Swift is held under 3.5 yards a carry, so Chicago becomes one-dimensional by halftime.

CHI 33–16 SEA 17% chance · avg margin 16.8

Ben Johnson's offense keeps humming: Chicago has the better offense on paper (+0.047 EPA/play, +0.106 per dropback), and Williams has thrown for 305 and 308 since returning. The blowout path starts up front. Montez Sweat (#98) and the debuting Jeffery Simmons (#99) feast on a Seattle line missing Charles Cross, and Sam Darnold (23rd in QBR) gives the ball away. What has to happen

  • Chicago turns red-zone trips into TDs (50% so far), where Seattle's defense has been weakest.
  • Seattle's new three-back split stalls, putting the game on Darnold's arm while trailing.

Method & caveats

  • Scenario probabilities come from the market (Pinnacle no-vig ML and spread, margin σ = 13.5), not from the app's models, which trail the closing line.
  • Player projections start from each prop's market median and apply rule-of-thumb game-script multipliers (the leading team runs more and passes less; the trailing team throws more). They are not fitted to data, so read them as directional.
  • Scenario legs are conditional bets: they only make sense if you believe that script is more likely than the market says. Unconditionally they are no better than the market price, and the measured edges are on the main analysis.
  • Seattle's RB projections are the shakiest: Charbonnet and Price return together and the split is unknown until the game starts.
